A dollar you stop spending is worth fifty-nine in sales.

FMI puts food-retail net profit at 1.7%.13 That's the whole arithmetic: a dollar of energy you never buy drops straight to the bottom line, and to match it on the floor you'd have to ring up fifty-nine more.

Meanwhile about a third of the energy an average commercial building buys does nothing at all.1 Most stores have never had anyone qualified find out where theirs goes.

You've been pitched savings before, by someone selling a single product, with a payback chart from a different store. Saying no was the right call. But the bills keep arriving, in every store, every month.

+See why those pitches fail

Most energy vendors carry one product, so every store gets the same answer. The case study they show you is a different building's prescription. And the people who know your equipment best, your refrigeration and HVAC service companies, are paid for service hours, not for kilowatt-hours you never use. Nobody in that model is paid to measure your stores first, so the waste survives the upgrade.

That is the entire difference here: the measurement comes first, and the fix is chosen to match what it finds, from more than four dozen technologies.

Your two biggest systems are fighting each other.

In South Florida the dew point outside works against your cases 365 days a year, not four. The rooftop units set the humidity in the store; the refrigeration racks pay for it. When the dew point in the aisle drifts up, glass doors fog, cases sweat, anti-sweat heaters switch on, and the racks defrost more often — all of it electricity, none of it colder food.

Those two systems are almost never serviced by the same company, and neither one is measured against the other. Two contractors, two invoices, one dew point, nobody refereeing. It is the most expensive argument in the building, and it never appears as a line item anywhere.

We measure both sides of it before anyone proposes equipment. Cheap dehumidification upstream is routinely worth more than expensive refrigeration downstream, and you cannot know which until someone reads both.

The cold never clocks out.

At close, the lights dim, the registers go quiet, and the rooftops set back for the night. The refrigeration doesn't. It holds your product through every dark hour, which is why it's the one load that runs the entire year — and the one place waste compounds around the clock.12

8,760hours a year the compressors never stop. A rooftop rests half the day; a refrigeration circuit never gets to.

This is why we start with the meter, not the roof: the fix that pays most in a store is usually the one attached to the load that never gets to rest.

Your worst store is hiding in the average.

Across your 32 stores, Hialeah to Orlando — same banner, same planogram, the same package on the roof — the spread between your best store and your worst routinely runs into double digits per square foot. And Orlando's climate isn't Miami's. Corporate sees one number. The stores bleeding inside it are invisible.

Nobody ranks them, because ranking them means pulling twelve months of interval data from every meter in the fleet, and it is nobody's job to go get it. It is the first thing we do, before a single piece of equipment is discussed.
